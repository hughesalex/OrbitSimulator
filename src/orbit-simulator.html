    Orbit Simulator

    You are flying a spacecraft around Earth (or, from the Start menu, round the Moon, Mars or Ganymede). Nothing pushes it except gravity, so it falls around the planet on a fixed path: an ellipse (or, if it is going fast enough, an escape path that never returns). The only way to change that path is to fire the engine, and the direction you fire in matters as much as how hard.

    The six burn directions

    • Prograde / retrograde: along / against your direction of travel. Changes your speed, so it raises or lowers the opposite side of the orbit.
    • Normal / anti-normal: at right angles to the orbit's plane. Tilts the orbit, changing its inclination.
    • Radial out / in: away from / towards Earth. Rotates the ellipse around you without changing its size much.

    Planning a burn

    1. Click your orbit to drop a manoeuvre node there.
    2. Drag the coloured handles on the node (or use the buttons in the Manoeuvre panel). The dashed orange path shows where you would go after the burn, and the Orbit panel shows the new numbers beside the current ones.
    3. Press Execute to jump to the node and fire. Or let the clock run: it stops at the node, and the Play button becomes Fire burn, so pressing it (or Space) fires the burn and carries on. Total Δv (change in velocity) is the "fuel" every mission budgets for.
    4. Not what you wanted? Undo burn puts the craft back where it was and the node back on the plan, so you can adjust it. It works for burns flown by hand too, and after a crash.

    Plans with several burns

    Add up to six burns. Each one is planned from where the burns before it leave you, so it only works if they go as planned. The Manoeuvre panel lists the plan in time order: burns (round markers, like the nodes on the map) fire the engine; events (diamonds) are just things the path does on its own, such as reaching the Moon's sphere of influence or passing closest to it. Click a burn to edit it: the Orbit panel then shows the orbit just after it. Click an event to see the orbit at that point.

    Plan it for me can put you in a circular orbit round whichever body the plan ends at, tilt your orbit (to a number, or into another body's orbital plane), or take you to any of the planets or their major moons with Transfer to, and into orbit there if you tick stay in orbit. Where there is more than one way, it lays them side by side with the fuel (Δv) and the time each takes, so you can see what a quicker trip costs. Click one to see it on the map, then Keep this one.

    The Moon, the planets and their moons

    The Moon circles Earth every 27.3 days, about 384,000 km out. Inside its sphere of influence (the dashed ring round it) its gravity takes over, and your path bends round it; outside, only Earth counts. The same goes all the way up: outside Earth's sphere of influence (about 925,000 km) only the Sun pulls, and inside Jupiter's only Jupiter, until you reach one of its moons. This is the patched-conic method Kerbal Space Program uses. A planned path that reaches another body shows a faint copy of it where it will be when you arrive, and the part of your path round it is drawn about that copy.

    Getting to the planets

    Launch windows. The cheapest way from one planet to another is close to a Hohmann transfer: half an ellipse round the Sun that just touches both orbits. It only works if the target will be at the far end when you get there, so it can only start when the two planets are lined up just right, which happens once every synodic period: every 2 years 50 days for Earth and Mars, every 1 year 219 days for Venus, every 13 months for Jupiter. Leave at the wrong time and the fuel bill can double.

    Fuel against time. A quicker path is a fatter ellipse that cuts across the target's orbit at a steeper angle, so you pay twice: more to leave, and more to stop when you get there. The porkchop plot under each plan shows the fuel for every departure date (across) and trip time (up), brighter where it takes less fuel: the bright islands are the launch windows. The numbered rings on it are the ways listed above it. Click a ring to show that way, or anywhere else to plan that departure.

    Burn low. You leave from a low parking orbit because a burn where you move fastest adds the most energy (the Oberth effect): about 3.6 km/s from low Earth orbit sends you on to Mars, though Mars needs only about 2.9 km/s on top of escape speed far from Earth.

    Going inward is not downhill. Earth carries you round the Sun at 30 km/s, and to fall toward the Sun you must cancel some of that. From low Earth orbit, stopping at Mercury costs more than twice what it takes just to reach Jupiter. The idea of going outward first and burning at aphelion, far out where you move slowly, is a bi-elliptic transfer, and it only pays when the two orbits differ in size by about 12 times or more. Mercury's orbit is only 2.6 times smaller than Earth's, so for Mercury it costs more; but to fall into the Sun itself it saves fuel, and the planner shows both side by side. Real missions to Mercury and the outer planets use gravity assists, swinging past other planets on the way; the planner leaves those out for now.

    The planner goes one step at a time, from a body to its neighbour, its parent or its moons: to reach Titan from Earth, transfer to Saturn first and plan again from there.

    What the fuel means for a real rocket

    The rocket equation. A rocket speeds up by throwing fuel out of the back, so the Δv it can reach depends on how fast its exhaust leaves and how much of it is fuel. For every tonne that arrives you need eΔv/ve tonnes at departure, where ve is the exhaust speed: the fuel grows exponentially with the Δv. With a hydrogen/oxygen engine (4.4 km/s exhaust), 4.4 km/s of Δv needs 2.7 tonnes at departure per tonne that arrives; twice that needs 7.4 tonnes, and three times that 20. Pick an Engine above the ways on offer to see what each one would take: green is routine today, amber needs a big rocket or several stages, and red is not practical with that engine. Nothing is ever ruled out, so you can still fly a red plan and see why no one does.

    Staging. Tanks and engines weigh something too, and a single stage can carry only about nine tonnes of fuel per tonne of everything else. So rockets come in stages, dropping each empty one so the next does not have to push it. That is why the Saturn V had three, and why a Mars probe rides a separate upper stage before using its own small engine.

    Cheating the equation. Real missions avoid much of the Δv shown here. Aerobraking uses a planet's upper atmosphere to slow down for free instead of braking with the engine (Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter did most of its slowing this way). Gravity assists steal speed from a planet as you swing past it: Voyager, Cassini and New Horizons could never have carried the fuel to go direct. Neither is in this simulator yet.

    Fast exhaust, gentle push. An ion engine's exhaust is ten times faster than chemistry, so it needs far less fuel, but its push is like the weight of a sheet of paper. Here every burn is instant; an ion engine would take weeks or months for the same change, spiralling slowly outward, and that slow climb out of a planet's pull costs more Δv than an instant burn deep in it.

    Planes and launch windows

    Planes shows the plane of your orbit (gold) and the plane of the Moon's orbit (blue) as discs through Earth, the angle between them, and the dashed line of nodes where they cross. A cheap transfer stays in your plane, so it can only meet the Moon where the Moon's path crosses it: the markers show when the Moon next gets there. That is why a Moon transfer from a tilted orbit can mean waiting days.

    Heading for another planet, the target's own plane matters little (the planets' orbits are within a few degrees of each other). What matters is the way out: the direction you must leave Earth's pull in for the launch window, shown as an orange arrow. Planes then compares your orbit with the departure plane, the plane through Earth that holds the way out and is nearest yours, and says what the tilt between them adds to the burn to leave. Launching into the departure plane, at the right time of day, makes it zero. Once you have picked a plan, the arrow shows that plan's own way out.

    Launch… starts from the ground at Cape Canaveral, Baikonur, Kourou, Vostochny, Wenchang, Jiuquan, Tanegashima or Sriharikota. Pick where you're going first. For Earth orbit you launch straight away, due east (the least tilt, equal to the site's latitude) or at a steeper tilt that costs more. For the Moon you get a list of launch times, about two a day, each one setting up a parking orbit whose plane will hold the Moon when you arrive; the planner then lays out the ways to get there.

    Getting a better look

    3D, Top-down (from above the North Pole) and Face-on (square to the orbit's plane) swing the camera round; Fit zooms to the whole orbit and plan. Follow keeps the craft, the Sun or any planet or moon in the centre as it moves; click a body's label on the map to follow it. Zoom out far enough and you see the whole Solar System, with a dated line ahead of the craft as it crosses between the planets. Following Earth, the path bends where it leaves Earth's influence: inside, it is drawn around Earth, at the few km/s you move relative to it; beyond, it is drawn around the Sun, where Earth's own 30 km/s round the Sun is added to that, so the line swings to follow Earth's orbit. The bend is where the drawing changes viewpoint, not a burn. If the burn to leave is a long way off, say at the next launch window to Mars, Earth will have moved on by then: the way out is drawn round a faint copy of Earth labelled "Earth when you leave", so the path round the Sun sits exactly where it will be, against the orbit lines. Follow the Sun to see the whole path round it at once.

    Things to try

    • Raise the far side. From low Earth orbit, add a node and pull prograde to +500 m/s. Watch apoapsis climb on the opposite side of Earth while the node point stays put.
    • Hohmann transfer. From the equatorial parking orbit, plan a Hohmann transfer to 35,786 km (geostationary height). It takes two burns, about 2.4 and 1.5 km/s, half an orbit apart.
    • Plane changes are expensive. From the ISS-like orbit, plan an inclination change to 0°. Then compare doing it from far out at a transfer orbit's apoapsis, where you move slowly.
    • Fly to the Moon. From the equatorial parking orbit, pick the Moon under Transfer to. The slow way takes about 3.1 km/s and a 5 day coast, then about 0.8 km/s to brake into orbit at the Moon. Compare the quicker transfers: they arrive faster, which makes braking dearer. Untick stay in orbit and the Moon flings you onto a new orbit round Earth instead: a gravity assist.
    • Come home. From the low lunar orbit, transfer to Earth. Braking into orbit at Earth costs about 3 km/s more than the trip itself, which is why Apollo let the atmosphere do it.
    • Waiting for a window. Try the same from the ISS-like orbit. Its tilt differs from the Moon's, so the planner waits days until the Moon comes round to where your orbit's plane crosses its path.
    • Launch to the Moon. Press Launch…, pick Baikonur and The Moon, and launch at the first time offered. Compare with the ISS-like start: the slow transfer now leaves within the hour instead of days later. Press Planes to see why: the line of nodes points to where the Moon will be.
    • Mars, and the window. From the equatorial parking orbit, transfer to Mars. A window opens in late 2026, and the plan lists the cheapest trip, the faster ones and their cost. Open Why these ways? and the porkchop plot, then click a point outside the bright island to see what leaving at the wrong time costs.
    • Mercury: easy to reach, hard to stop at. From the same orbit, plan Mercury with stay in orbit unticked, then ticked. Reaching it costs about 5 km/s, but you arrive so fast that stopping more than doubles the bill. Compare the bi-elliptic way, out to Jupiter's distance first.
    • Down to the Sun. Untick stay in orbit and pick the Sun under Transfer to, to dive within 6 million km of it, as Parker Solar Probe does. Falling straight in costs about 16 km/s from the equatorial orbit; going out to 20 AU first and braking there costs under 10 km/s, but takes 33 years.
    • A tour of Jupiter's moons. Start round Ganymede and transfer to Europa, then Callisto: the same windows and trade-offs, in days instead of years.
    • Match planes. From the ISS-like orbit, choose the Moon's orbital plane under Change inclination to and plan it: one burn where the planes cross.
    • The Oberth effect. Put 100 m/s prograde at periapsis of the Molniya orbit, then the same at apoapsis. Burning where you are fastest changes your orbital energy far more.
